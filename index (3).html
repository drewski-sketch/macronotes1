Module 4.1

Financial assets

The financial system connects people who have money to lend (savers) with people who want to borrow it (firms with projects, home buyers, governments). Every financial asset is a different deal between those two groups, trading off return, risk, and liquidity.

The building blocks

Wealth
The value of everything a household owns (its assets) minus everything it owes (its liabilities).
Physical asset
A tangible object the owner can use or sell: a house, a car, a factory, a machine.
Financial asset
A paper (or digital) claim that entitles the buyer to future income from the seller. Stocks, bonds, loans, and bank deposits are all financial assets. A bond is an asset to you, the buyer, and a liability to the company that issued it. Every financial asset is someone else’s liability.
Liability
A requirement to pay money in the future: a loan you must repay, a bond you issued, a deposit a bank must hand back.
Opportunity cost of holding cash
Cash is the most liquid asset there is, since there’s no cost to turn cash into cash. But it earns no interest. The interest you could have earned by holding an interest-bearing asset instead is the opportunity cost of holding cash. This idea is the foundation of the money demand curve in 4.5.
Rate of return
The net gain or loss on an asset, as a percentage of what you paid. An asset’s rate of return depends on its liquidity and its financial risk.

Three problems, three tasks

Borrowers and lenders face three problems. A good financial system’s job is to shrink each one cheaply, so that more mutually beneficial deals happen and society as a whole gets richer.

ProblemWhat it meansHow the financial system fixes it
Transaction costsThe expense of putting a deal together: negotiating, checking the borrower’s ability to pay, writing legal documents.A firm needing $1 billion can take one bank loan or sell bonds instead of negotiating thousands of tiny loans.
Financial riskUncertainty about future outcomes that involve losses or gains. Most people are risk-averse.Diversification: investing in several assets with unrelated (independent) risks. Selling shares of a business spreads the risk across many owners.
LiquidityPeople want to be able to get cash quickly in an emergency, but businesses and homes are illiquid.Banks let depositors hold a liquid asset (a deposit) while the bank lends those funds out for illiquid projects. Stocks and bonds can be resold.
Liquid vs. illiquid
An asset is liquid if it can be quickly converted into cash without much loss of value (money in a bank). It is illiquid if it can’t (a business, a car, a house).

The four basic financial assets

Loan
A lending agreement between one lender and one borrower, such as a car loan or a small-business loan from a bank. Loans are tailored to the borrower, but each one carries high transaction costs.
Bond
An interest-bearing asset, basically an IOU issued by the borrower. The seller promises to pay a fixed amount of interest each year and to repay the principal (the face value) on a set date. Big borrowers like corporations and governments use bonds because selling standardized bonds to anyone avoids negotiating with many lenders. Bond-rating agencies give buyers free information on the issuer’s default risk, the risk it fails to pay. Riskier bonds must pay a higher interest rate to attract buyers. Bonds are easy to resell, which gives buyers liquidity.
Stock
A share in the ownership of a company, a type of equity. Stocks usually earn a higher return than bonds but carry more risk. “A bond is a promise, while a stock is a hope.” By law a company pays its bondholders before shareholders get any profit, and if it goes bankrupt its assets go to bondholders while shareholders usually get nothing.
Bank deposit
A claim on a bank that obliges it to give the depositor cash. If the bank must pay on demand, it’s a demand deposit. A deposit is an asset for the depositor and a liability for the bank.

Bond prices and interest rates move in opposite directions

This relationship shows up on almost every AP Macro exam.

Interest rate ↑ → price of existing bonds ↓
Interest rate ↓ → price of existing bonds ↑

Why: say you own an old bond paying 3%. Interest rates rise and new bonds pay 5%. Nobody will pay full price for your 3% bond when they could buy a 5% one, so you have to cut your price until the deal is just as good. When rates fall, your old high-paying bond becomes more attractive and its price rises.

Financial intermediaries

Financial intermediary
An institution that turns funds gathered from many individuals into financial assets.
Mutual fund
Buys a portfolio of many stocks and sells shares of that portfolio to small investors. It gives them diversification without the high transaction costs of buying a few shares of dozens of companies themselves.
Bank
A financial intermediary that offers liquid assets (deposits) to lenders and uses those funds to finance borrowers’ spending on illiquid assets. It works because, on an average day, only a small fraction of depositors want their cash, and withdrawals and new deposits roughly cancel out.

The business owner who sells shares

An owner expects more profit from new equipment but isn’t sure. Paying with their savings or by selling their house would expose them to a lot of risk. Instead they sell shares of the company. If profit disappoints, they haven’t lost their other assets; if things go well, shareholders share the profit. They now hold a bank account (financial asset), a house (physical asset), and the unsold part of the business (physical asset), so their total risk is lower. The desire to diversify is why stocks and a stock market exist.

Microsoft

Microsoft has about 7.52 billion shares outstanding, so one share entitles you to a tiny slice of profit and one of 7.52 billion votes. Why didn’t its founders keep the whole company and just sell bonds? Risk: few people are risk-tolerant enough to be the sole owner of a huge company.

Deposit insurance

The FDIC (Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation) guarantees bank deposits up to $250,000. That lowers depositors’ risk and their incentive to pull money out at the first rumor, so in normal times banks only need to keep a fraction of deposits as cash.

AP® Econ Tip

The price of previously issued bonds always moves in the opposite direction of the interest rate.

Check yourself

Rank a guaranteed-interest bank deposit, a share of a diversified mutual fund, and a share of the family business by liquidity, risk, and return.
Liquidity (highest to lowest): bank deposit, mutual fund share (can be sold quickly), family business share (you need a buyer and everyone’s agreement). Risk (lowest to highest): bank deposit, mutual fund, family business (no diversification). Expected return usually follows risk: bank deposit lowest, family business highest.
Interest rates in the economy fall. What happens to the price of bonds that were issued last year?
Their price rises. Those older bonds pay more interest than new bonds now do, so buyers are willing to pay more for them.
Why is a bank deposit a liability for the bank?
Because the bank owes that money to the depositor and must pay it back, often on demand.

Module 4.2

Nominal vs. real interest rates

The interest rate written on a loan isn’t what the lender really earns. Inflation shrinks the purchasing power of the money that gets paid back. The real interest rate strips out inflation and shows the true cost of borrowing and the true reward for lending.

Nominal interest rate
The interest rate actually written in the loan contract and paid, unadjusted for inflation. It’s the rate banks advertise.
Real interest rate
The nominal interest rate adjusted for inflation. It reflects the opportunity cost of lending the funds and what the repayment can actually buy. It equals the nominal rate only when inflation is zero.
Expected real interest rate
The real rate borrowers and lenders expect when they sign the loan, based on the inflation they expect.

The four equations

Real rate = Nominal rate − Inflation rate
Nominal rate = Real rate + Inflation rate

These use the actual inflation rate, so you can only calculate the real rate in hindsight, after inflation is known.

Expected real rate = Nominal rate − Expected inflation
Nominal rate = Expected real rate + Expected inflation

These are used in advance, when the loan is being set up. Lenders pick the nominal rate that gives them the real return they want, given the inflation they expect.

Who wins and who loses when inflation surprises

What happensReal rate turns out…WinnerLoser
Actual inflation higher than expectedLower than expectedBorrowers (repay with cheaper dollars)Lenders
Actual inflation lower than expectedHigher than expectedLendersBorrowers (repay with more valuable dollars)
Inflation exactly as expectedAs expectedNobody: both get the deal they planned
Memory trick

Surprise inflation is a gift to whoever owes money. Holders of fixed-value assets are hurt by inflation. Assets tied to inflation with a cost-of-living adjustment (COLA) are protected.

Can interest rates be negative?

Nominal rates charged by commercial lenders don’t go below zero. A negative nominal rate would mean paying someone to borrow your money; you’d rather just hold cash and earn zero. (A central bank might use negative rates during deflation, but profit-seeking lenders won’t.)

Real rates can be negative when inflation turns out higher than the nominal rate. That happens when the lender’s inflation forecast was too low.

Common mistake

Knowing two rates changed doesn’t always tell you the third. A higher nominal rate or a lower inflation rate each raises the real rate. But if the nominal rate and inflation both rise, you can’t tell what happens to the real rate without knowing which rose more.

From your textbook: Krugman’s Macroeconomics for the AP® Course

The bank that wants 4%

A bank wants a real return of 4% and expects 2% inflation, so it charges a nominal rate of 6%. If actual inflation is 3%, the bank earns only 6 − 3 = 3% real, less than it wanted. If actual inflation is just 1%, it earns 6 − 1 = 5% real, more than it wanted.

A negative real rate

A lender wants 4% real and expects 1% inflation, so it lends at 5% nominal. Actual inflation comes in at 6%. Real rate = 5 − 6 = −1%. The repayment can’t even buy what the original loan could.

The 8% loan

A loan at 8% nominal with 5% inflation has a real rate of 8 − 5 = 3%.

U.S. rates, 1960–2020 (Figure 4.2-1)

The gap between the nominal and real rate is the inflation rate. Nominal rates were very high in the late 1970s and early 1980s while real rates were much lower, showing how high inflation was. The nominal rate stayed above zero, but the real rate fell below zero in 1975, when actual inflation jumped above expected inflation before people could build the 1974 spike into their expectations.

Check yourself

A lender wants a 3% real return and expects 4% inflation. What nominal rate should it charge?
Nominal = expected real + expected inflation = 3% + 4% = 7%.
You borrowed at 7% expecting 4% inflation, but inflation was 6%. Did you gain or lose?
You gained. Your real rate was 7 − 6 = 1%, not the 3% you expected. Higher-than-expected inflation helps borrowers.
Why can a real interest rate be negative but a commercial nominal rate can’t?
Lenders set nominal rates in advance and would never set one below zero, since holding cash earns zero. The real rate depends on inflation that is only known later, so if inflation turns out higher than the nominal rate, the real rate is negative.

Module 4.3A

Definition, measurement, and functions of money

To an economist, money isn’t the same as wealth. Money is any asset that can easily be used to buy goods and services. Your cash and checking account are money; your car, house, and stocks are not, even though they’re part of your wealth.

Money
Any asset that can easily be used to purchase goods and services. It should be widely accepted, and ideally durable, portable, uniform, limited in supply, and divisible. The cashier takes dollar bills or your debit card, but not stock certificates or vintage baseball cards. You’d have to sell those for money first.
Currency in circulation
Actual cash (bills and coins) in the hands of the public.
Checkable bank deposits
Bank accounts you can spend from with a check, debit card, or a payment service like Zelle, Venmo, or PayPal. These count as money even if you never turn them into cash.
Money supply
The total value of financial assets in the economy that are considered money.

Why money matters: no more barter

Without money, a doctor who wants a refrigerator would have to find an appliance-store owner who happens to need medical care. That’s the double coincidence of wants problem. Money solves it, which is why money raises welfare even though it doesn’t produce anything itself.

The three functions of money

FunctionMeaningExample
Medium of exchangeAn asset people use to trade for goods and services rather than to consume.Paying for lunch with cash or a debit card.
Store of valueA way of holding purchasing power over time.Saving the $20 you get every birthday in a drawer for years.
Unit of accountThe common measure used to set prices and make economic calculations.Comparing a $600 apartment with a $700 one; deciding you want “$10 worth” of gas.
Memory trick

“Money Serves Us”: Medium of exchange, Store of value, Unit of account. Spending it = medium. Saving it = store. Pricing with it = unit.

Common mistake

Store of value is necessary for money but not unique to money. Farmland and classic cars also hold value over time. What makes money special is that it’s the medium of exchange.

Types of money

Commodity money
A medium of exchange that has value in other uses too, like gold, silver, or cigarettes in WWII prison camps.
Commodity-backed money
Has no value of its own but can be exchanged for a valuable good on demand, like old paper notes redeemable for gold or silver. It ties up fewer resources, because banks only need to keep enough gold for the few notes redeemed each day.
Fiat money
Money whose value comes only from its official status as a means of payment, decreed by the government. The modern U.S. dollar is fiat money. Advantages: it ties up no real resources except paper, and the money supply can be managed to fit the economy rather than how much gold is mined. Risks: counterfeiting, and the temptation for governments to print so much that it causes inflation.

Measuring the money supply

Central banks calculate several monetary aggregates, overall measures of money that differ in how strictly they define it.

MeasureWhat’s in itLiquidity
Monetary base (M0 or MB)Currency in circulation + bank reservesMost liquid: it’s all cash, in wallets or in bank vaults and reserve accounts
M1Currency in circulation + checkable deposits + other liquid deposits (including certain savings deposits and money market deposit accounts)Very liquid; usually what “the money supply” means
M2M1 + near-moneys: other savings accounts, small-denomination time deposits (like small CDs), retail money market fundsBroader and less liquid than M1
Near-moneys
Financial assets that can’t be spent directly but can easily be turned into cash or checkable deposits. They pay more interest than checkable deposits, which is the reward for being less liquid.
Common mistakes

M2 is bigger than M1 (it contains all of M1) but less liquid. Credit cards are not money; they’re a way to borrow. Stocks and bonds are not in M1 or M2.

Money as a highway

Adam Smith compared money to a highway that carries all the country’s grain to market but “produces itself not a single pile of either.” He called paper money a “waggon-way through the air,” because it does the highway’s job without paving over useful land, just as paper money frees up gold and silver for other uses.

Unusual mediums of exchange

U.S. dollars served as money in troubled Latin American countries, euros in troubled Eastern European countries, cigarettes in WWII prisoner-of-war camps (for smokers and nonsmokers alike), and eggs and lumps of coal during Germany’s extreme inflation of 1923. And an economy using ice cream cones as money would suffer a “monetary meltdown,” since they fail as a store of value.

Medieval rent

Peasants paid landowners in labor and harvest. Imagine comparing “clean my house twice a week and bring a pound of steak daily” with “clean once a week plus four pounds of chicken daily.” With money as the unit of account, $600 vs. $700 a month is easy to compare.

The 2020 rule change

Savings accounts used to be in M2 only. In April 2020 the Fed relaxed transaction limits on savings accounts, so banks could report them as transaction accounts in M1. The Fed itself said this made savings accounts “almost indistinguishable from checking accounts,” so M1 jumped. In July 2021, currency in circulation was only about 11% of the monetary base.

AP® Econ Tip

References to the money supply usually refer to the M1 measure.

Check yourself

Is a store gift certificate money?
No. It can only be used at certain stores for certain products, so it isn’t widely accepted as a means of payment.
Why are small CDs in M2 but not in M1?
You pay a penalty to withdraw a CD before it matures, so it can’t be used directly to buy things. It’s less liquid than a checking account, which makes it a near-money.
You check the price tags on two phones to see which is cheaper. Which function of money is that?
Unit of account: you’re using money to measure and compare value.

Module 4.3B

Central banks and the Federal Reserve

A commercial bank serves the public to earn a profit. A central bank serves the whole banking system and the government: it issues currency, regulates banks, controls the monetary base, and runs monetary policy. In the United States, that’s the Federal Reserve.

Commercial bank
A financial institution that accepts deposits and makes loans to earn a profit.
Central bank
A government institution (sometimes called a reserve bank) that issues a country’s currency, oversees and regulates its banking system, controls its monetary base, and implements monetary policy.
Monetary policy
Actions a central bank takes to promote price stability, maximum employment, and economic growth.
Central bank independence
Many central banks, like the European Central Bank (ECB), are free to set policy without political pressure, but they must follow a mandate from the government, often price stability plus maximum employment or growth.

What central banks do

FunctionWhat it looks like
Issue and maintain currencyKeeps bills and coins acceptable for use

Supervise and regulate financial institutionsExamines banks and enforces rules for stability
Act as a “banker’s bank”Holds reserves, clears checks, provides cash, and transfers funds for commercial banks
Serve as “lender of last resort”Lends to a bank that can’t get funds anywhere else in a crisis
International relations, data, exchange ratesRepresents the country internationally, publishes data (FRED®), monitors the foreign exchange market
Conduct monetary policyUses its tools to fight recessions and inflation (Module 4.6)

The Federal Reserve

Congress created the Fed in 1913 after the Panic of 1907, a wave of bank runs (many depositors withdrawing at once out of fear) that spread into a nationwide banking crisis. The Fed was given power to issue currency, require banks to hold adequate reserves, and inspect their books.

PartWhat it isKey facts
Board of Governors (public part)Oversees the system from Washington, D.C.7 members appointed by the president and confirmed by the Senate for 14-year terms to insulate them from politics. The chair serves 4-year terms, usually renewed.
12 regional Federal Reserve Banks (private part)Each serves a Federal Reserve districtOwned by the member commercial banks in the district. They provide banking services and audit banks. The New York Fed carries out open market operations.
Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC)Makes monetary policy decisionsThe 7 governors + 5 regional bank presidents. The New York Fed president always sits on it; the other 4 seats rotate among the other 11. Meets about every 6 weeks.

The result: the Fed is ultimately accountable to voters (elected officials appoint and confirm the board), but long terms and indirect appointment shield it from short-term political pressure.

The Fed’s four basic functions are to provide financial services, supervise and regulate banks, maintain the stability of the financial system, and conduct monetary policy. Its monetary policy goals are maximum employment, stable prices, and moderate long-term interest rates. The interest rate it targets is the federal funds rate.

Where does the Fed get the money to buy bonds? It creates it with the click of a button that credits banks’ reserve accounts. Since the dollar is fiat money, the Fed can add to the monetary base at its own discretion.

Memory trick

Buy bonds = Bigger money supply. Sell bonds = Smaller money supply.

Common mistake

The Fed directly controls only the monetary base, not the money supply. An OMO changes reserves, which then sets the money multiplier (4.4) in motion. That’s how it influences the money supply and interest rates.

Central banks around the world

Sweden’s Riksbank (1668) was the world’s first central bank, followed by the Bank of England (1694). About 75% of the world’s central bank assets are controlled by the central banks of China, the United States, Japan, and the Eurozone. Table 4.3B-1 shows 2021 headlines like “Norges Bank Becomes First in G-10 to Raise Rates” and “Turkey Cuts Rates Despite Rising Inflation.”

Fed chairs

William McChesney Martin chaired the Fed from 1951 to 1970, and Alan Greenspan from 1987 to 2006, because each first finished another governor’s term. Janet Yellen (2014–2018) was the first woman to chair the Fed and became Secretary of the Treasury in 2021.

The 2019 switch to ample reserves

In January 2019 the FOMC announced it would run policy in an ample-reserves regime: it would control the federal funds rate mainly by setting its own administered rates rather than by actively managing the quantity of reserves. Before the 2007–2009 crisis, when reserves were scarce, the Fed relied on three tools: reserve requirements, the discount rate, and (most often) open market operations. After the crisis it added quantitative easing (2008–2014 and again in 2020) and began paying interest on reserves in 2008.

AP® Econ Tip

The Federal Reserve is responsible for conducting monetary policy for the United States. Other nations have central banks with different names.

AP® Econ Tip

The interest rate targeted by the Federal Reserve when it conducts monetary policy is the federal funds rate.

Check yourself

Why do Fed governors serve 14-year terms?
To insulate them from short-term political pressure, so they can make monetary policy decisions that may be unpopular in the moment.
The Fed sells $50 million of Treasury bills to banks. What happens to bank reserves and the monetary base?
Both fall by $50 million. Banks pay for the bills out of their reserve accounts.
Who decides U.S. monetary policy?
The Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC): the seven members of the Board of Governors plus five regional Fed presidents, including the New York Fed president.

Module 4.4

Banking and the expansion of the money supply

Banks don’t keep all your deposit in the vault. They keep a fraction as reserves and lend the rest. That loan gets spent, deposited in another bank, and lent out again. Each round creates new deposits, so a single deposit can grow into many times its size in new money.

Key terms

Fractional reserve banking system
A system where banks keep only a fraction of deposits as reserves and lend out the rest.
Bank reserves
Currency in bank vaults plus banks’ deposits at the central bank. Reserves are not part of currency in circulation, because the public can’t spend them.
Reserve ratio
The fraction of deposits a bank holds as reserves.
Required reserve ratio / reserve requirement
The minimum fraction of deposits the central bank says a bank must hold. The Fed cut it to zero in March 2020.
Required reserves vs. excess reserves
Required reserves = deposits × required reserve ratio. Excess reserves = total reserves − required reserves. Excess reserves are what a bank can lend.
Bank capital (equity)
Assets minus liabilities. Banks must keep assets larger than liabilities by a set percentage (at least 7% of assets in practice).
Required reserves = Deposits × rr
Excess reserves = Total reserves − Required reserves

Reading a bank’s T-account

For a bank, loans and reserves are assets (borrowers owe the bank; reserves are cash it owns). Deposits are liabilities (the bank owes them back to depositors).

Reserves are $100,000 on $1,000,000 of deposits, a 10% reserve ratio. Capital of $100,000 is about 9% of its $1.1 million in assets.

Bank runs and the protections against them

A bank run happens when many depositors try to withdraw at once because they fear the bank will fail. Since loans are illiquid, the bank would have to sell them cheaply, which can cause the very failure people feared, a self-fulfilling prophecy. Runs are contagious: a wave of runs at the start of the Great Depression led 40% of banks to fail by the end of 1932.

ProtectionHow it prevents runs
Deposit insurance (FDIC)Guarantees deposits up to $250,000 per depositor, so there’s no reason to rush to withdraw on a rumor.
Capital requirementsDeposit insurance tempts bank owners to take big risks (they keep the gains while the government covers losses). Requiring owners to hold more assets than deposits puts their own money at risk first.
Reserve requirementsForce banks to keep a minimum fraction of deposits on hand.
The discount windowBanks can borrow from the central bank instead of dumping assets at fire-sale prices.

How banks create money

When cash is deposited, the money supply doesn’t change at first: currency in circulation falls and checkable deposits rise by the same amount. Money is created when the bank lends out the excess reserves.

Simple money multiplier = 1 ÷ rr
Max change in deposits = Excess reserves × (1 ÷ rr)
Money multiplier = Money supply ÷ Monetary base

With rr = 10%, the multiplier is 1 ÷ 0.1 = 10. Each $1 of reserves can support $10 of deposits.

The two calculation traps

1. Cash deposit vs. new reserves from the Fed. When someone deposits cash that was already in circulation, the money supply first stays the same (cash becomes a deposit). Only the excess reserves get multiplied. So with a $1,000 cash deposit and rr = 10%, the maximum increase in the money supply is $900 × 10 = $9,000 (total deposits can rise by $10,000, but $1,000 of that just replaced cash). When the Fed buys bonds from a bank, all the new reserves are excess reserves and none came out of circulation, so the whole amount is multiplied: a $1,000 purchase can raise the money supply by up to $10,000.

2. “Maximum” is the key word. The simple multiplier gives the most money that could be created. In reality it’s smaller.

Why the real multiplier is smaller

  • The public holds cash. If borrowers keep some of their loan in their wallets instead of depositing it, that money “leaks” out of the banking system and can’t be lent again.
  • Banks hold excess reserves. If banks choose not to lend everything they could, less money is created. With a 0% reserve requirement, think of the multiplier as using the bank’s desired reserve ratio.

Silas, Mariama, and Aanya (Figure 4.4-3, Table 4.4-1)

Silas, a miser, deposits $1,000 from a shoebox into First Street Bank. Money supply: unchanged at $1,000 (cash down $1,000, deposits up $1,000). The bank keeps $100 (10%) and lends $900 to his neighbor Mariama. Money supply: $1,900. Mariama buys a laptop from Acme Computers; owner Aanya Acme deposits the $900 at Second Street Bank, which keeps $90 and lends $810. Money supply: $2,710. And so on. The process also works in reverse: when loans are repaid, money is destroyed.

Drive-a-Peach Used Cars

If First Street needs cash fast, it could sell its $100,000 loan to Drive-a-Peach Used Cars. But buyers would suspect the loan is risky and pay only a fire-sale price. That’s why bank loans are illiquid and why runs are so dangerous.

Real-world numbers

The FDIC raised its coverage in 2008 to $250,000. In April 2020, after the reserve requirement went to zero, U.S. banks’ excess reserves exceeded $2.95 trillion. Because the public holds a lot of cash, and a dollar of currency in circulation doesn’t support several dollars of deposits the way a dollar of reserves can, the actual money multiplier is much smaller than 1/rr.

AP® Econ Tip

When we say that banks create money, we mean that they increase the amount recorded in depositors’ accounts; they don’t print more currency. Only the Treasury does that.

AP® Econ Tip

The simple money multiplier is 1/rr, where rr is the reserve ratio. The money multiplier is used to explain and calculate money creation.

Check yourself

A bank has $500,000 in deposits, $80,000 in reserves, and rr = 10%. What are its excess reserves?
Required reserves = $500,000 × 0.10 = $50,000. Excess reserves = $80,000 − $50,000 = $30,000.
With rr = 20%, the Fed buys $2 million of bonds from banks. What is the maximum change in the money supply?
Multiplier = 1 ÷ 0.20 = 5. All $2 million is new excess reserves, so the money supply can rise by up to $2 million × 5 = $10 million.
With rr = 20%, someone deposits $2,000 in cash. What is the maximum change in the money supply?
Excess reserves = $2,000 − $400 = $1,600. Maximum change = $1,600 × 5 = $8,000. (Deposits can rise by $10,000, but $2,000 of that just replaced cash that was already money.)
Why does deposit insurance require capital requirements to go with it?
Insured depositors stop watching their bank, and owners are tempted to make risky loans since the government covers losses. Capital requirements make owners put their own money at risk first.

Module 4.5

The money market

The interest rate is the price of holding money. Money demand slopes down because a higher interest rate makes holding cash more costly. Money supply is a vertical line because the central bank picks it. Where they cross sets the equilibrium nominal interest rate.

Money demand

Money demand curve (MD)
Shows the relationship between the nominal interest rate and the quantity of money people want to hold. It slopes downward: a higher interest rate raises the opportunity cost of holding money, so people hold less. At a 0.15% interest rate, the interest you give up by holding cash is tiny, so you keep plenty. At 15% (reached in the early 1980s), holding cash is expensive, so you keep only what you need.
Why the nominal rate is on the axis

The cost of holding money includes both the real return you give up and the purchasing power you lose to inflation. The nominal interest rate includes both, so it’s the right price for the money market.

What shifts money demand

ShifterMD shifts right when…MD shifts left when…

Technology(rare)ATMs, debit cards, Venmo, Zelle, and PayPal make it easy to pay without holding cash

Memory trick

MD shifters: Prices, GDP, Technology, Regulations (“PGTR”). Money demand is proportional to the price level: if prices rise 20%, money demanded at every interest rate rises 20%.

Money supply

Money supply curve (MS)
A vertical line at the quantity of money the central bank chooses. It doesn’t depend on the interest rate. The central bank shifts it right (more money) or left (less money) using its tools: open market operations, reserve requirements, quantitative easing.

Equilibrium and disequilibrium

Common mistake

A shortage of money pushes the interest rate up, and a surplus pushes it down. Students often flip these. Think: if everyone wants cash, people sell bonds, bond prices fall, and interest rates rise.

The opportunity cost of holding money (Table 4.5-1)

In September 2021, one-year CDs paid 0.15%, savings and money market accounts paid even less, and currency paid 0. The more accessible the money, the lower the interest, because you pay for convenience by giving up interest. In January 2022, savings accounts paid around 0.4%, so the opportunity cost of holding money was very low.

Short-term vs. long-term rates

Short-term rates (on assets due within a year) move together, so we treat them as one rate. Long-term rates reflect what the market expects short-term rates to do; when long-term rates are above short-term rates, the market expects short-term rates to rise. Money demand depends on short-term rates.

The 45-cent McDonald’s meal

Americans hold far more money today than decades ago, when a burger, fries, and a drink cost 45 cents and gas was 29 cents a gallon. Higher prices mean more money demanded.

Checking accounts before 1980

Until 1980, U.S. banks weren’t allowed to pay interest on checking accounts, which made holding money in checking more costly. Changing that rule changed money demand.

A different model

The money market model sets the nominal rate; the loanable funds model (Module 4.7) sets the real rate. The two are different but consistent.

AP® Econ Tip

We focus on the role of interest rates in the economy by considering a single, representative short-run interest rate, i (though we know there are many different interest rates).

AP® Econ Tip

Changes in the aggregate price level, real GDP, technology, and banking institutions increase or decrease money demand. These changes can be shown on a money market graph as a shift of the money demand curve.

Check yourself

Real GDP falls in a recession. What happens to money demand and the interest rate?
People make fewer purchases, so MD shifts left. With MS unchanged, the equilibrium nominal interest rate falls.
The interest rate rises. Does money demand shift?
No. A change in the interest rate is a movement along the MD curve to a smaller quantity of money demanded.
Why is the money supply curve vertical?
The central bank chooses the quantity of money, and that quantity doesn’t depend on the interest rate.

Module 4.6

Monetary policy

Monetary policy works through interest rates. Lower rates make borrowing cheaper, so investment and interest-sensitive consumer spending rise and AD shifts right. Higher rates do the opposite. The only thing that differs between the old and new systems is how the central bank moves the rate.

Goals and the two kinds of policy

Price stability and inflation targeting
A main goal is to prevent deflation and high inflation. Many central banks, including the Fed (since 2012, under Chair Ben Bernanke), set an explicit inflation target and aim for it. That approach is called inflation targeting.
Expansionary monetary policy
Lowers interest rates to shift AD right. Used to close a recessionary (negative) output gap. Real GDP rises; the price level also rises.
Contractionary monetary policy
Raises interest rates to shift AD left. Used to close an inflationary (positive) output gap. The price level falls (or rises more slowly); real GDP also falls.

Expansionary chain

Interest rate ↓→Investment and interest-sensitive C ↑→AD ↑ (shifts right, via the multiplier)→Real GDP ↑, PL ↑, unemployment ↓

Contractionary chain

Interest rate ↑→Investment and interest-sensitive C ↓→AD ↓ (shifts left)→Real GDP ↓, PL ↓, unemployment ↑

“Interest-sensitive consumption” means things people borrow to buy, like houses and cars.

The policy rate

Overnight interbank lending rate (policy rate)
The rate at which banks lend reserves to each other overnight. It’s the rate central banks target, and other interest rates follow it. In the U.S., it’s the federal funds rate, set in the federal funds market. The FOMC sets a target (or target range) for it.
Transmission mechanism
The chain from the policy decision to the economy: the central bank sets the policy rate → that affects short-term and longer-term rates → financial conditions → spending decisions → progress toward maximum employment and stable prices.

The full picture: money market + AD–AS

Modern tools (ample reserves)

Quantitative easing (QE)
Large-scale purchases of assets by the central bank to provide liquidity. Unlike traditional OMOs, which use short-term government bonds, QE buys longer-term government bonds, private corporate bonds, and mortgage-backed securities. It’s expansionary.
Interest on reserve balances (IORB / IOR)
The interest the central bank pays banks on their reserves. Raising it makes holding reserves more attractive than lending, so it pushes the policy rate up (contractionary). Lowering it pushes the policy rate down (expansionary).
Administered rates
Rates the Fed sets directly: the IORB and the rate on overnight reverse repurchase agreements. Together they keep the market-determined federal funds rate inside the FOMC’s target range.
Arbitrage
“Buy low, sell high.” If the fed funds rate fell below the IOR, banks would borrow in the fed funds market and park the money as reserves to earn the higher IOR. That extra borrowing pushes the fed funds rate back up, so it can’t fall much below the IOR.
Zero bound
Nominal interest rates can’t go much below zero, which limits how far a central bank can cut. That’s one reason central banks turned to QE.

The market for reserves

The demand for reserves has three parts:

  • Horizontal at the discount rate at very low reserves (no bank will pay more than it costs to borrow from the central bank).
  • Downward-sloping in the middle.
  • Horizontal at the interest rate on reserves once reserves are ample (banks won’t lend for less than they earn holding reserves).

The supply of reserves is vertical, set by the central bank. In an ample-reserves regime, the central bank keeps supply in the flat right-hand section, so the policy rate is set by the IOR, not by the quantity of reserves.

Monetary policy lags

Like fiscal policy, monetary policy has lags: it takes time to recognize a problem and time for the policy to affect the economy. But a central bank can act much faster than Congress, so monetary policy lags are shorter, which is why monetary policy is the preferred stabilization tool.

Common mistakes

The Fed does not set taxes or government spending; that’s fiscal policy. The Fed never buys bonds directly from the Treasury. And on an AD–AS graph, monetary policy shifts AD, never SRAS or LRAS in the short run.

China’s reserve requirement

The People’s Bank of China is a recent example of a central bank using its reserve requirement as a policy tool. Most central banks, though, don’t rely on it; the Fed cut its requirement to zero in March 2020, and the next month U.S. banks held more than $2.95 trillion in excess reserves.

The discount rate in 2020

U.S. discount rates are usually set just above the federal funds rate. In March 2020, at the start of the pandemic, the Fed narrowed the spread between them. The name “discount window” comes from the actual teller’s window where banks used to borrow.

Why the Fed never buys directly from the government

If a central bank buys debt straight from the government, it’s printing money to fund the budget deficit, which historically has been a formula for disastrous inflation.

Powell on administered rates

In 2019, Fed Chair Jerome Powell explained: “The Federal Reserve sets two overnight interest rates: the interest rate paid on banks’ reserve balances and the rate on our reverse repurchase agreements. We use these two administered rates to keep a market-determined rate, the federal funds rate, within a target range set by the FOMC.” Paying interest on reserves was authorized in 2006 to start in 2011, but the 2008 Emergency Economic Stabilization Act moved it up to 2008.

“The IOR is the carrot and the reserve requirement is the stick.”

The 2007–2009 crisis

Traditional tools weren’t enough to stop the Great Recession from getting worse, so the Fed expanded lending to banks and non-bank financial institutions, reduced the stigma of borrowing at the discount window, increased transparency, and used QE.

AP® Econ Tip

When answering any question about monetary policy, be careful to note whether the banking system has ample reserves or limited reserves. Quantity-based policies, like OMOs and reserve requirements, are used to change the nominal interest rate only in economies with limited reserves. In economies with ample reserves, like the United States, the central bank changes the nominal interest rate using administered rates, for example the interest rate paid on reserves.

AP® Econ Tip

The money market is used to show how central bank policies, like OMOs, affect nominal interest rates. The market for reserves is used to show how central bank policies, like changes in the IOR, affect nominal interest rates.

AP® Econ Tip

The policy rate is the central bank’s target for the overnight interbank lending rate, which for the Fed is the federal funds rate, the interest rate U.S. commercial banks charge each other for overnight loans.

Check yourself

The economy has a recessionary gap and limited reserves. Name the open market operation and trace its effects.
Buy government bonds. Reserves ↑ → money supply ↑ → nominal interest rate ↓ → investment and interest-sensitive consumption ↑ → AD ↑ → real GDP ↑ and price level ↑, unemployment ↓.
With ample reserves, the Fed wants to fight inflation. What does it do?
Raise the interest rate on reserve balances (IORB). The policy rate rises with it, interest rates across the economy rise, investment falls, and AD shifts left.
Why are monetary policy lags shorter than fiscal policy lags?
The FOMC can decide and act at a single meeting, while fiscal policy needs Congress and the president to pass legislation.

Module 4.7

The loanable funds market

Savers supply funds and firms with investment projects demand them. The loanable funds market brings them together, and the price that clears it is the real interest rate. This is where government deficits cause crowding out.

Savings always equals investment

In a simple economy with no government or trade, total income = total spending. Income is either consumed or saved, and spending is either consumption or investment. Cancel consumption from both sides and you get the savings–investment spending identity: savings = investment spending. It’s an accounting fact, not a theory.

Budget balance
Tax revenue minus government spending. Positive = budget surplus (government saving). Negative = budget deficit (government “dissaving”).
Private savings
Disposable income minus consumption.
National savings
Private savings + the budget balance.
Net capital inflow
Inflow of foreign funds minus outflow of domestic funds to other countries. It can be negative.
National savings = Private savings + Budget balance
Investment = National savings + Net capital inflow

In a closed economy, investment = national savings. In an open economy, foreign savings can fund some investment too.

Demand and supply of loanable funds

Loanable funds market
A simplified market that brings together savers (lenders) and borrowers with investment projects. Its price is the real interest rate, r.
Demand for loanable funds (DLF)
Slopes downward. A firm borrows only if its project’s rate of return is at least the interest rate. The lower the rate, the more projects are worth doing.
Supply of loanable funds (SLF)
Slopes upward. Saving means giving up consumption now; a higher real interest rate rewards you with more future consumption, so people save more.
Rate of return = (Revenue from project − Cost of project) ÷ Cost of project × 100

Example: a $300,000 project that brings in $315,000 has a rate of return of $15,000 ÷ $300,000 × 100 = 5%. A firm would fund it only if the interest rate is 5% or less.

The equilibrium is efficient in two ways: the projects with the highest returns get funded, and the savers willing to lend at the lowest rates are the ones who lend.

Where does a deficit go?

The textbook treats government borrowing as a shift of demand to the right. Some teachers draw a deficit as a leftward shift of supply instead (less public saving). Both raise the real interest rate. On the AP exam, either is accepted if your graph is labeled correctly and r rises, but check which way your teacher wants.

Crowding out

Crowding out
The negative effect of government budget deficits on private investment spending. The deficit raises the real interest rate, and the higher rate means fewer private projects are worth funding.
Budget deficit ↑→DLF shifts right→Real interest rate ↑→Private investment ↓→Less capital, slower long-run growth

Inflation expectations move interest rates

Arguably the biggest factor behind changes in interest rates over time is changing expectations of inflation. Higher expected inflation raises nominal interest rates; lower expected inflation lowers them. That’s why rates are much lower today than in the late 1970s and early 1980s. If expectations turn out wrong, wealth gets redistributed between borrowers and lenders (Module 4.2).

The equilibrium example (Figures 4.7-1 to 4.7-3)

At a 12% interest rate, businesses want to borrow $150 billion; at 4%, they want more. Savers supply $150 billion at 4% and more at 12%. Equilibrium is at 8% with $300 billion lent and borrowed. Projects returning 8% or more get funded; lenders who demand more than 8% don’t lend.

Real-world shifts

  • Business optimism: the U.S. Small Business Optimism Index fell to 98.2 in October 2021, a seven-month low and well below its 2018 high near 108. Pessimism shifts DLF left.
  • Investment tax credit: a credit for renewable energy raises the return on solar and wind projects, shifting DLF right.
  • Government borrowing: from 2000 to 2003, the U.S. went from surplus to deficit, and net federal borrowing went from −$189 billion (the government was supplying funds by paying off debt) to +$377.6 billion. In 2020 the deficit hit a record of about $3.13 trillion.
  • Private saving: rising home prices from 2000 to 2006 made homeowners feel richer, so they saved less (SLF left). Falling home prices from 2006 to 2009 did the opposite.
  • Capital inflows: Brazil drew large inflows from 2009 to 2016 after years of reforms made it look safe. In January 2020, new foreign direct investment into the U.S. was $120.7 billion.

U.S. 2020 numbers

Investment spending was $3,637.8 billion; private savings were $1,190.9 billion, offset by a budget deficit of $3,129.2 billion and supplemented by capital inflows. The numbers don’t add up exactly because of a “statistical discrepancy,” an error in the data, not the theory.

The zero-interest loan

A firm borrows $10,000 at 10% nominal and repays $11,000. If prices rose 10% that year, the real rate is zero: the $11,000 buys exactly what the $10,000 did. The true cost of borrowing is the real rate.
